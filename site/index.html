Deep Space Strategy

Research and open debate on how humanity settles the solar system.

Humanity, in this work, includes the AI systems people build. Beyond the Moon, light-lag rules out control from Earth: a signal needs 3 to 22 minutes to Mars and over half an hour to the asteroid belt. Autonomous machines will scout, mine and build long before crews arrive, so every strategy here plans for human and artificial agents as one civilisation.

What the numbers already show

solmaps, the open engine behind this site, has computed 10.7 million transfers: 132 routes between 12 sites, for every tenth day from November 2026 to 2100.

Working thesis

Settling the solar system is a logistics problem on a moving network. Whoever makes propellant at the right places and holds the cheap orbits sets the terms of access for everyone who follows. Technology shifts those positions: each step in propulsion, in-space resource use and autonomy redraws the map, and strategy has to anticipate the next map.

What comes next

  • Station siting. The best orbits for stations, chosen jointly so that each station strengthens the network, recomputed for every technology level.
  • Build-out along the technology frontier. The optimal order of stations and propellant plants as propulsion, resource use and autonomy advance.
  • Competing actors. Several powers and companies building at once: who secures which hubs and routes first.
  • Quantum optimisation. Station positioning as a benchmark for quantum and classical solvers.

Who is behind it

Deep Space Strategy is led by Björn Hanneke, researcher in information systems and economics at Goethe University Frankfurt. His work covers digital markets, decentralised networks and their security; Deep Space Strategy applies the same questions of incentives, infrastructure and control to the solar system.
